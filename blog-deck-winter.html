How to Protect Your Deck Through Indiana's Freeze-Thaw Winter

The cold itself doesn't destroy decks. The cycle of freeze, thaw, and refreeze does — and most of the damage is preventable.

Maintenance November 5, 2025 7 min read

Indiana winters are not gentle. In Lake, LaPorte, and Porter counties, the ground can freeze to 36 inches deep, and we routinely cycle through temperatures that drop below zero and climb back above freezing within the same week. That repeated freeze-thaw is the primary enemy of any outdoor wood structure — more damaging than a single deep freeze ever could be.

The good news is that a properly built and maintained deck handles Indiana winters without issue. The damage you see — split boards, heaved posts, rusted fasteners, rotten ledgers — is almost always the result of either poor construction or deferred maintenance, not the weather itself.

Here's what you need to do and what to watch for.

Why Freeze-Thaw Damages Decks

Wood is porous. It absorbs moisture constantly — from rain, snow, morning dew, and standing water. When that moisture freezes inside the wood fibers, it expands. When it thaws, it contracts. Do that 30 or 40 times in a single winter and you're running a stress test on every board, every joint, and every fastener.

The damage compounds fast:

  • Boards crack along the grain and begin to check (surface splits)
  • Fasteners — nails, screws, and joist hangers — loosen as wood swells and shrinks around them
  • Post bases and concrete footings can crack if water infiltrates and freezes inside them
  • The ledger board (the board bolted to your house) can rot from trapped moisture, eventually compromising the structural connection between deck and home

None of this is inevitable. Sealed wood resists moisture absorption. Frost-proof footings prevent heave. The right fasteners don't rust. The issue is that a lot of decks in the region weren't built to those standards, or were built right but never maintained.

End-of-Season Cleaning

Before the first hard freeze, your deck needs a thorough cleaning. This is the most important preventive step and the one most homeowners skip.

  • Clear debris: Wet leaves are the primary culprit for surface rot and mold. They hold moisture against the wood for weeks at a time. Sweep them off completely — don't let them sit until spring
  • Clean between boards: Use a stiff brush or a specialized deck tool to clear debris from the gaps between deck boards. Packed debris holds moisture and accelerates rot at the edges
  • Power wash: A low-pressure power wash (1,200–1,500 PSI for wood; softer for composite) removes mold, mildew, and surface grime. Allow the wood to dry fully — at least 48 hours — before applying any sealer
  • Check around planter pots and furniture: These create moisture traps wherever they sit. Move them, clean underneath, and store planters for winter

Sealing and Staining — When and What

A quality deck sealer is a moisture barrier. It's the single most effective thing you can do to slow freeze-thaw damage on a wood deck. For Indiana's climate, the timing and product choice both matter.

  • When to seal: Late September through mid-October is ideal — after the summer humidity drops and before overnight temperatures stay below 50°F consistently. Most deck sealers need temperatures above 50°F to cure properly
  • How often: Every 2–3 years for a penetrating oil-based sealer; annually for a water-based sealer or if your deck gets heavy foot traffic or direct sun
  • What to use: Penetrating oil sealers (such as TWP or Armstrong Clark) outperform film-forming sealers in freeze-thaw climates. Film-forming products peel and trap moisture beneath the surface — exactly what you don't want
  • New decks: Wait at least 6 months before sealing new pressure-treated lumber. The treatment chemicals need to fully dry and off-gas or the sealer won't bond properly

Snow Removal — What's Safe and What Damages Your Deck

Snow removal is where a lot of well-intentioned homeowners inadvertently cause damage.

  • Use a plastic shovel: Metal shovels scratch and gouge wood and composite decking. A plastic blade removes snow without damaging the surface
  • Shovel with the grain: On wood decks, push parallel to the boards — not across them — to avoid catching board edges and splintering
  • Don't use rock salt: Sodium chloride (standard road salt) accelerates corrosion on metal fasteners and can damage wood fibers and composite surfaces. Use calcium chloride or magnesium chloride — they're safer for decking materials and work at lower temperatures
  • Leave a thin layer: Don't scrape all the way to bare decking. A thin layer of snow actually insulates the surface. The goal is to prevent ice buildup, not to get the deck down to bare wood
  • Don't use a snow blower: The metal auger housing can contact the decking surface and the discharge chute can damage furniture, railings, and fixtures nearby

What to Inspect Every Spring

Once temperatures stabilize above freezing for good, do a systematic walkthrough of your deck. You're looking for evidence of what the winter found and what it made worse.

  • Split and checked boards: Surface checks (cracks along the grain) are cosmetic. Deep splits that run the full depth of a board are structural and need replacement
  • Raised fasteners: Screw or nail heads that have pushed up above the surface indicate freeze-thaw movement in the boards. Drive them back in or replace with longer fasteners
  • Post bases and footings: Check for cracking in the concrete around post bases. Press on each post — any movement suggests the footing has heaved or shifted
  • Ledger board: Look at where the deck attaches to the house. Discoloration, soft spots, or visible rot at the ledger connection is a serious structural issue that needs immediate attention
  • Railing wobble: Push on all railing sections. Loose railings are a safety issue, not just cosmetic. Most loosen because the fasteners at the post base have worked themselves loose through seasonal movement
  • Rust staining: Brown streaks running down from fastener heads indicate the fasteners are corroding. This is common on older decks built before corrosion-resistant fasteners became standard

What a Well-Built Deck Handles on Its Own

A deck built to Indiana standards — frost-proof concrete footings poured below the 36-inch frost line, corrosion-resistant hardware, properly spaced boards for drainage — handles our winters without issue year after year. You maintain the surface with cleaning and sealing; the structure takes care of itself.
