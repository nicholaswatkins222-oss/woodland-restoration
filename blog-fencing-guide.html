How to Choose the Right Fencing for Your Property

The most common fencing mistake isn't a bad installation — it's choosing the wrong material from the start.

Fencing October 14, 2025 6 min read

Most homeowners pick a fence based on looks or what the neighbor has. That's how you end up with a vinyl privacy fence that buckles after three Indiana winters, or a wood fence that needs repainting every two years because you didn't account for the humidity. The right fence comes down to matching the material to your specific goals — not just aesthetics, but function, lifespan, and how much ongoing work you're willing to put in.

Here's a straight breakdown of the four main fence types we install and who each one is actually right for.

Chain Link — Practical, Proven, and Underrated

Chain link gets dismissed as industrial, but it earns its place in more situations than people admit. It's the right call when your primary goal is containment or perimeter definition — not privacy. Dog owners, homeowners with large lots, and agricultural properties all benefit from it.

  • Cost: Lowest upfront cost of any fence type, by a significant margin
  • Lifespan: Galvanized chain link lasts 20–30 years with almost zero maintenance
  • Best for: Pet containment, large perimeter runs, agricultural and farm use, commercial properties
  • Limitations: Provides no privacy and minimal curb appeal in residential settings

If you need to fence a half-acre lot for your dogs or define property lines on a farm, chain link is often the most honest choice. We install both residential and agricultural chain link across Lake, LaPorte, and Porter counties.

Wood — The Classic Choice That Requires Commitment

Wood fencing is the most popular choice for residential privacy, and for good reason. A well-built cedar or pressure-treated fence looks sharp, blends naturally into landscaping, and gives you full control over design — height, spacing, style, and finish.

But wood demands more from you than any other fence material. Here's what you're actually signing up for:

  • Pressure-treated pine: Most affordable wood option, greenish tint fades to gray over time, needs sealing every 2–3 years to prevent rot and cracking
  • Western cedar: Naturally rot-resistant, cleaner look, holds stain better than treated pine — but costs more upfront
  • Lifespan: 15–25 years with proper maintenance; significantly less if neglected
  • Maintenance: Expect to power wash and reseal every 2–3 years; boards will eventually split, warp, or need replacing

Wood fencing is a great investment if you're willing to maintain it. If you want something you can install and largely ignore, it isn't the right call.

Wrought Iron & Ornamental Steel — Security Meets Curb Appeal

Wrought iron and ornamental steel fencing are the premium choice for homeowners who want a strong visual statement along with serious security. These fences don't provide privacy — the open design is the point — but they define a boundary with authority.

  • Durability: Properly coated steel or iron lasts 50+ years with minimal upkeep
  • Maintenance: Watch for rust at welded joints; touch up with rust-inhibiting paint every few years
  • Best for: Front yard accents, pool enclosures, driveways, property entrances
  • Cost: Higher than wood or chain link — but you're paying for lifespan and impact

Paired with a custom gate, ornamental steel fencing makes a property look finished and intentional. It's a common choice for the front of the property with wood privacy fencing on the sides and back.

Vinyl — Low Maintenance, High Upfront Cost

Vinyl fencing is sold heavily on the "no maintenance" pitch. That's mostly true — vinyl doesn't rot, doesn't need painting, and holds its color for years. But it comes with trade-offs that often get glossed over at the sales stage.

  • Durability concerns: Vinyl becomes brittle in sustained cold. In Indiana, where we see extended freeze-thaw cycles, poor-quality vinyl panels crack and warp more often than the marketing suggests
  • Cost: Significantly more expensive than wood upfront — often 2x or more
  • Repairability: When panels crack or posts shift, repairs are harder and more expensive than wood
  • Best for: Pool enclosures, decorative fencing, homeowners who specifically want zero maintenance and buy quality product

If you go vinyl, invest in commercial-grade material. The budget vinyl sold at big box stores is what gives the product a bad reputation in cold climates.

Indiana-Specific Considerations

Fencing in the Midwest isn't the same as fencing in a temperate climate, and any contractor who doesn't mention the following shouldn't be setting your posts.

  • Frost depth: Indiana frost depth runs 24–36 inches in northern counties. Posts set shallower than that will heave — shifting the entire fence line within a few winters
  • Wind load: Open farmland and large lots in Lake and Porter counties see sustained wind. Tall privacy fences need proper post sizing and concrete footing to handle it
  • HOA rules: Many subdivisions in the region have restrictions on height, material, and front-yard fencing. Always check before you build
  • Permits: Woodland handles all permit applications — we won't start a job that needs one until it's in hand

How to Decide

Work through these three questions:

  1. What is the fence's primary job? Privacy, containment, security, curb appeal, or some combination?
  2. How much ongoing maintenance are you willing to do? Be honest. Wood is beautiful but demanding. Chain link and ornamental steel are nearly maintenance-free.
  3. What's the full-cost horizon? Cheaper upfront isn't cheaper over 20 years if you're repainting every two years or replacing panels after a hard winter.
